# Automotive Safe-IC Practice Articles

This directory contains the article series for **Automotive Safe-IC Practice**: a staged engineering walkthrough for building a reproducible functional-safety automation flow around automotive chip verification evidence.

The series is organized as a 20-stage path. It starts from analysis input packaging and FIT assumptions, then moves through structural safety context, safety-mechanism mapping, fault-list generation, fault-campaign preparation, result classification, final metric writeback, FMEDA modeling, diagnostic-coverage closure, CI-style regression gating, evidence traceability, and an end-to-end mini-flow review packet.

The intended reader is an engineer who wants to understand not only *what* artifacts a functional-safety flow produces, but also *how* those artifacts can be connected into a repeatable evidence chain.

---

## Repository Context

This `articles/` directory is the long-form technical explanation layer of the project.

A typical stage has three related assets:

1. **Article** — explains the engineering motivation, concepts, architecture, and methodology.
2. **Demo package** — provides a reproducible mini-example for the stage.
3. **Evidence outputs** — CSV, Markdown, report, database-session, or dashboard artifacts generated by the demo.

The articles should be read as an engineering narrative, not as isolated tool notes. Later stages intentionally consume evidence produced by earlier stages.

## Core Concepts

### Functional Safety

Functional safety means the system remains free from unacceptable risk caused by malfunctioning behavior of electrical, electronic, or programmable electronic systems. In automotive chip practice, this usually requires connecting design structure, failure modes, safety mechanisms, fault-campaign results, diagnostic coverage, and review evidence.

### FIT and BFR

**FIT** means failures in time, usually expressed as failures per one billion operating hours. **BFR** means base failure rate. The flow treats FIT/BFR values as engineering assumptions that must be traceable from standard selection and mission-profile assumptions into final metrics and FMEDA tables.

### Safety Mechanism

A safety mechanism is a design feature that detects, controls, mitigates, or prevents the impact of a fault. Examples include parity, ECC, duplication, lockstep, watchdogs, timeout logic, range checks, protocol checks, and alarm logic.

### Fault Campaign

A fault campaign is a structured set of injected faults used to evaluate whether faults are detected, observed, masked, safe, unsafe, or unresolved. The campaign is not just a simulator run; it is a managed experiment with input lists, observation rules, classification policy, and result traceability.

### Good Machine

The good machine is the reference behavior without injected faults. Fault-campaign classification depends on comparing faulty behavior against this reference behavior within the intended observation window.

### FTTI

**FTTI** means fault tolerant time interval. It is the time window within which a fault must be detected or controlled before it can lead to an unsafe effect.

### Observe Point

An observe point is a signal or state location used to evaluate whether a fault effect becomes visible. Observe points help distinguish “not propagated,” “propagated but safe,” and “propagated with possible safety impact.”

### Alarm

An alarm is a safety-relevant indicator generated by a safety mechanism. In a fault campaign, alarm behavior is used to decide whether a fault was detected by the intended safety mechanism.

### Diagnostic Coverage

Diagnostic coverage measures how effectively safety mechanisms detect or control faults. The series treats diagnostic coverage as both a metric and a traceability problem: every claimed coverage value should be connected to campaign evidence, classification logic, and FMEDA rows.

### FMEDA

FMEDA stands for Failure Modes, Effects, and Diagnostic Analysis. It connects failure modes, safety mechanisms, diagnostic coverage, residual FIT, and safety metric interpretation. In this project, FMEDA is treated as a structured data model rather than a manually edited spreadsheet only.

### SPFM, LFM, and PMHF

**SPFM** is single-point fault metric. **LFM** is latent fault metric. **PMHF** is probabilistic metric for random hardware failures. The project does not treat these metrics as isolated numbers; it connects them to evidence sources, closure status, and review gates.

### Evidence Traceability

Evidence traceability means every decision can be traced back to artifacts such as reports, CSVs, database sessions, logs, hash manifests, dashboards, and review actions. This is the central theme of the final stages.

## Engineering Architecture

At a high level, the project follows this architecture:

```text
Safety Analysis Inputs
        |
        v
FIT / BFR Assumption Layer
        |
        v
Structural and Endpoint Context
        |
        v
Safety Mechanism Mapping
        |
        v
Fault List and Campaign Input Package
        |
        v
Fault Campaign Execution Evidence
        |
        v
Outcome Classification
        |
        v
Final Metric Writeback
        |
        v
FMEDA Data Model
        |
        v
Closure Action Management
        |
        v
Regression Gate
        |
        v
Evidence Traceability
        |
        v
End-to-End Review Packet
```

The key engineering idea is to separate **evidence production**, **evidence interpretation**, and **review decision**:

- Evidence production creates reports, database sessions, campaign outputs, and CSV tables.
- Evidence interpretation classifies outcomes, computes rollups, and maps results to failure modes.
- Review decision turns unresolved items, unsafe candidates, and metric gaps into closure actions and gates.

## Evidence Philosophy

A functional-safety workflow should not hide imperfect results. If a fault is unresolved, if a safety mechanism binding is incomplete, or if a high-priority closure action remains open, the flow should carry that fact forward.

This is why later stages may produce a **WARN** decision instead of a simple **PASS**. A WARN state can be valuable when it is structured, traceable, and actionable.

Good evidence is not only positive evidence. Good evidence also records:

- what is known,
- what is unknown,
- what is unresolved,
- who or what should handle it next,
- which artifact proves the current state,
- and which downstream stage consumes it.

## Practical Use Cases

This article series is useful for several engineering scenarios:

### Small and Mid-size Chip Teams

Many teams have commercial EDA tools but lack the automation layer around safety evidence. The series shows how to structure input packages, campaign evidence, FMEDA tables, closure actions, and CI gates.

### EDA Infrastructure Engineers

The flow demonstrates how to build orchestration around analysis engines, database-backed evidence, report extraction, and regression-quality checks.

### DFT and Verification Engineers

Fault campaigns require DFT thinking, simulation infrastructure, classification policy, and waveform/observation discipline. The series connects those pieces into a safety-oriented flow.

### Functional Safety Engineers

FMEDA, diagnostic coverage, residual FIT, SPFM, LFM, PMHF, and safety mechanisms are treated as connected data rather than disconnected spreadsheet columns.

### Technical Reviewers

The final stages provide dashboards, traceability tables, and review packets that help reviewers understand what evidence supports each decision.

## What This Series Is Not

This series is not a certification claim, a signoff report, or a replacement for commercial safety tools. It is an engineering practice flow that demonstrates how to organize and connect safety evidence.

The project is intentionally transparent about review-level artifacts, unresolved items, and remaining closure work. That is closer to real safety engineering than a demo where everything is artificially green.
